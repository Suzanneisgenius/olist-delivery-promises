Sharper forecasts, longer promises




Sharper forecasts, longer promises:
calibration transfer in delivery-date policies
  ​Oct 21, 2026        · ​@geniussuzane


1. Summary
A sharper forecasting model did not give shorter promised delivery dates, because the
calibration learned in one period carried over differently to the next.

I started this project wanting to predict which Olist orders would arrive late. Along the
way I realised that "late" compares two things: how long delivery takes, and how generous
the promised date is. The question became: when does a better forecast actually give a
better promised date? The data are public Olist e-commerce orders (Brazil, 2017–2018),
restricted to single-seller orders that were eventually delivered. A promise is a whole
number of days; an order is covered if it arrives in time; the target coverage is 90%.

I compared a per-state quantile rule, a linear quantile regression and a shallow gradient-
boosted model (HGB) under a protocol written down before any new model was fitted.
Three findings:

 1. The comparison design changed the verdict. In the project's earlier comparison,
    regression promised 2.57 more days than the state rule. With the same training data,
    the same calibration procedure and the same feature timing, it promised 1.54 fewer
    days at 0.31 points lower coverage.
 2. HGB is the sharper model, yet its calibrated promise was longer. At equal coverage
    HGB needs fewer days than the linear model: 0.14 days fewer on the calibration period
     and 0.6–0.9 days fewer on the evaluation period (post hoc). But the shift learned on the
     calibration period carried HGB to 97.1% coverage on the evaluation period, against
     96.2% for the linear model. Its registered promise was therefore 1.22 days longer.
 3. No policy met the pre-set bar for replacing the existing rule. This verdict rests on the
     calibration level. The rule gives no credit for extra coverage, and at the existing rule's
     own coverage both models need fewer days (post hoc).

The evaluation orders had already been examined earlier in the project, so this is a pre-
registered exploratory study, not a blind test. Every analysis added after the scoring run is
labelled post hoc and changes no registered verdict.

2. Loss and decision: why a quantile, not the mean
If a late day and a spare day cost different amounts, the best promise is a quantile of the
delivery time, not its mean. Let Y be the delivery time in whole days and d the promise,
with the loss

L(d, Y) = c_late · (Y − d)₊ + c_slack · (d − Y)₊.

Let R(d) = E[L(d, Y)] and F the distribution function of Y. Moving the promise from d to d +
1 saves c_late on every order with Y > d and adds c_slack on every order with Y ≤ d.
Therefore

R(d + 1) − R(d) = (c_late + c_slack) · F(d) − c_late.

This difference never decreases in d, so R is minimised at the smallest whole d with F(d) ≥
q, where q = c_late / (c_late + c_slack). That is the empirical quantile rule the project uses
(NumPy's inverted_cdf). The pinball loss at level q is this loss with c_late = q and c_slack =
1 − q (scikit-learn); a 90% target thus corresponds to a 9 : 1 penalty ratio. The mean
minimises squared error and the median a 1 : 1 absolute loss; both treat a late day and a
spare day alike.

A check on validation data. On the 12,045 validation orders I gave every order the same
promise and computed the 9 : 1 loss for d = 1, …, 60. The minimum is at d = 20, the empirical
90% quantile: F(19) = 89.6% < 90% ≤ F(20) = 91.4%. Promising the mean (11.43, rounded
up to 12) would have covered only 65.5% of orders, at a loss of 23.76 per order against
15.61.

What 9 : 1 does not mean. It follows from choosing q = 0.90; I did not measure it, and it is
not Olist's real cost ratio. Customer harm may not grow linearly with late days; if one late
day already does most of the damage, a coverage constraint is closer to the truth than a
linear cost. Therefore the study reports the trade-off between coverage and promised
days. It does not claim a profit-optimal date.


3. Comparison design
A fair comparison changes only the method. In the fair comparison, each calibrated policy
used the same training orders, the same label cut-off, the same rule for seller-history
features and the same calibration procedure; the uncalibrated controls (B1 and the
retained rule B0) are kept apart. A label counts only if the order was delivered before the
decision date.

Calibration. Each calibrated policy is shifted by its own number computed on C: the k-th
smallest of its residuals y − ŷ, with k = ⌈(n + 1) · 0.9⌉ and n = 17,056. The promise is then
rounded up: max{1, ⌈ŷ + shift⌉. This has the form of split conformal, but its guarantee
needs C and E to be exchangeable, and C had a much higher late rate than E (Barber et
al.). Section 5 explains why rounding after the shift gives continuous models extra slack.

Stepwise comparison. The earlier comparison (R0) differed in three conditions at once,
so I changed one condition per step. The pre-set primary comparison is R3 against R0; R1
and R2 are descriptive.

Under this fixed order, changing the design alone reversed the judgement; the largest
step is giving the state rule the same calibration shift (+6 days). This is design sensitivity,
not a causal statement about which method is better. Two references are kept apart: the
fair reference B1c (state rule on F with the same calibration) and the replacement
reference B0 (the project's retained rule, fitted on F + C without a shift).

6. Limits and conclusion
Limits. One evaluation period, already examined earlier in the project; a conditional
population (single-seller, eventually delivered orders); a small stepwise model search;
approximate intervals from 17 week clusters. Promised dates do not change logistics, so
nothing here shows faster delivery or happier customers.

Conclusion. The pre-registered verdicts stand: the fair design reversed the regression-
versus-rule judgement, no linear change was selected, HGB did not clear the 1-day bar,
and nothing replaced the existing rule. Read together with the sensitivity checks, the
lesson is narrower and more useful than "complex models do not help". HGB is the sharper
model, but a calibration shift learned in a peak season, rounded in a way that favours
integer rules, and evaluated on a window filtered by Olist's own dates, decided where
each policy landed. The next question is therefore about calibration, not about models: an
integer-aware shift and recalibration that tracks changing conditions, tested on a new,
separately registered evaluation.
