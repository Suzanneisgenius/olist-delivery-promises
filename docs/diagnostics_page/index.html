From a predicted quantile to a delivery promise: loss, calibration and coverage

I study how to set a delivery promise: a decision that trades spare days against late arrivals. The mean summarises delivery times; an asymmetric loss identifies the quantile that answers this decision.

I work through standard quantile and conformal results, then apply them to the project's calibration and rounding rules. The interactive examples use illustrative data; Table 4 reports Olist results. The proofs are in the mathematical note, and the notebook works the examples by hand.

  1. 1Why a promise is a quantile
  2. 2Why a predicted quantile still needs calibrating
  3. 3How whole days change the result

1Step 1. Why a delivery promise is a quantile

Let \(Y\) be the delivery time in days and \(d\) the promised number of days. I describe the two kinds of error with the loss

\[ L(d,Y)=c_{\mathrm{late}}\,(Y-d)_+ + c_{\mathrm{slack}}\,(d-Y)_+ , \]

where \((z)_+=\max(z,0)\) and \(c_{\mathrm{late}},c_{\mathrm{slack}}>0\).

  • When \(Y>d\), the order is late, and each late day costs \(c_{\mathrm{late}}\).
  • When \(Y\lt d\), the promise leaves days to spare, and each spare day costs \(c_{\mathrm{slack}}\).

The coefficients are decision weights, not measured economic costs.

Because the project compares dates in whole days, I take \(Y\) to be a nonnegative integer and \(d\) an integer, with \(\mathbb E[Y]\lt\infty\) so that the expected loss is finite. Extending a promise from \(d\) to \(d+1\) days can only do one of two things:

  • if \(Y\le d\), it adds a spare day, and the loss rises by \(c_{\mathrm{slack}}\);
  • if \(Y\ge d+1\), it removes a late day, and the loss falls by \(c_{\mathrm{late}}\).

So, with \(F(d)=P(Y\le d)\), the difference in expected loss between neighbouring promises is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb E[L(d+1,Y)]-\mathbb E[L(d,Y)]\\ &\quad= c_{\mathrm{slack}}F(d)-c_{\mathrm{late}}\{1-F(d)\}\\ &\quad= (c_{\mathrm{late}}+c_{\mathrm{slack}})\{F(d)-q\}, \end{aligned} \]

where

\[ q=\frac{c_{\mathrm{late}}}{c_{\mathrm{late}}+c_{\mathrm{slack}}}. \]

While \(F(d)\lt q\), one more day lowers the expected loss; once \(F(d)>q\), it raises it. One optimal whole-day promise is

the answer: a quantile \[ d^*=\min\{d: F(d)\ge q\}, \]

which is the \(q\)-quantile. Where \(F\) has a flat stretch the optimum need not be unique; in general a whole-day promise \(d\) is optimal exactly when

\[ F(d-1)\le q\le F(d). \]

A shorter promise trades spare days for a higher risk of late arrival. I evaluate both outcomes together.

Under this loss a 90% quantile corresponds to \(c_{\mathrm{late}}:c_{\mathrm{slack}}=9:1\). The project fixes 90% as its research target; Olist's economic cost ratio remains unestimated.

Given the information at checkout, \(X=x\), the same derivation gives the conditional quantile \(Q_q(Y\mid X=x)\). That is the role of quantile regression in this project: it lets different orders receive different promises.

2Step 2. Why a predicted quantile still needs calibrating

A model trained to predict the 90% quantile need not cover 90% of new orders: its form may not fit, and a finite sample adds error.

Fix the fitted predictor \(\widehat f\). For \(n\) calibration orders that were not used to fit it, define the one-sided residuals

\[ R_i=Y_i-\widehat f(X_i),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n, \]

sort them as \(R_{(1)}\le\cdots\le R_{(n)}\), and take

\[ k=\lceil (n+1)q\rceil,\quad s=R_{(k)},\quad U(x)=\widehat f(x)+s. \]

This assumes \(k\le n\). For \(k=n+1\), the finite-sample construction sets the upper bound to infinity.

Why \(n+1\) and not \(n\)?

The guarantee concerns \(n\) calibration residuals and one new residual. With the fitted model fixed, if these residuals are exchangeable and almost surely have no ties, the rank of the new residual among the \(n+1\) is uniform. Hence

why n + 1 \[ \begin{aligned} P\{Y_{\mathrm{new}}\le U(X_{\mathrm{new}})\} &= P\{R_{\mathrm{new}}\le R_{(k)}\}\\ &= \frac{k}{n+1}\ \ge\ q. \end{aligned} \]

Without ties this construction covers exactly \(k/(n+1)\). With ties, the coverage event, which includes equality, may be more conservative. The lower bound is the split conformal guarantee, Theorem 1a in Barber et al. [1].

The probability averages over both the calibration set and the new order. It does not say that each order has a 90% chance of arriving on time, and it is not the same guarantee for one calibration set once that set is fixed.

Check the residual rank directly

By the rule, at least \(k\) calibration residuals are no larger than \(R_{(k)}\), so

\[ \widehat{\mathrm{Coverage}}_C\ge\frac{k}{n}. \]

This follows from the construction itself. The converse fails: coverage above \(k/n\) does not show that the code is right, since a bound wrongly raised by 1,000 days would pass too. The direct check is to recompute the \(k\)-th residual independently and compare it, value by value, with the saved shift.

I check the implementation against the residual rank, then assess the assumptions separately on future data.

3Step 3. Why whole-day dates need separate treatment

The model can output a real-valued bound, but a delivery date is a whole number of days. If \(Y\) is an integer, then for any real \(U\)

the key identity \[ Y\le U\iff Y\le\lfloor U\rfloor, \]

because \(\lfloor U\rfloor\) is the largest integer not exceeding \(U\).

So, without further truncation or changes to the bound, rounding down keeps exactly the coverage event of the real-valued bound. Rounding up can cover some extra orders, and lengthens the promise.

But rounding down is not always the same as rounding up and then subtracting a day:

\[ \lfloor U\rfloor=\begin{cases} \lceil U\rceil-1, & U\notin\mathbb Z,\\ \lceil U\rceil, & U\in\mathbb Z. \end{cases} \]

For example, with \(U=10\) and \(Y=10\), the real-valued bound and rounding down both cover the order; rounding up and subtracting a day gives 9 and does not.

Policies produce whole-day bounds at different rates, so the same rounding step can affect them differently. I compare the complete rule, from prediction to the final date.

4Step 4. Assess coverage when the data change

In the project, the calibrated policies aimed at 90% covered about 96–97% of orders in the evaluation period. The gap raises a specific question: which parts of the promise-setting procedure produced the extra coverage?

First, the rank argument of step 2 relies on the calibration residuals and future residuals being exchangeable. Exchangeability between the calibration and evaluation periods remains unestablished; historical order alone neither establishes nor refutes it. I therefore report the evaluation coverage as an empirical result, with the theoretical guarantee conditional on its assumptions.

Second, the over-coverage could come from several places:

  • delivery times differing between the calibration and evaluation periods;
  • a different order mix or sample selection;
  • ties in the residuals;
  • whole-day rounding and other post-processing.

I use over-coverage to investigate these explanations. Identifying a cause requires evidence that separates them.

Going further: what the theory still says when exchangeability fails coverage bounds [1], weighted calibration [2] and Fig. 8

Two published results describe how coverage guarantees depend on the type of distribution shift.

Coverage bounds without exchangeability. Barber, Candès, Ramdas and Tibshirani [1] bound the coverage of split conformal calibration when the data are not exchangeable. Write \(Z\) for the calibration orders followed by the new order, \(Z^i\) for the same sequence with the new order swapped with calibration order \(i\), and \(R(Z)\) for the vector of the \(n+1\) residuals. For the rule used here, which weights every calibration order equally, their Theorem 2a gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\{Y_{n+1}\le U(X_{n+1})\}\\ &\quad\ge q-\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_{\mathrm{TV}}\bigl(R(Z),R(Z^i)\bigr), \end{aligned} \]

and, when the residuals are distinct with probability one, their Theorem 3 gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &P\{Y_{n+1}\le U(X_{n+1})\}\\ &\quad\lt q+\frac{1}{n+1}\\ &\qquad+\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_{\mathrm{TV}}\bigl(R(Z),R(Z^i)\bigr). \end{aligned} \]

Under exchangeability every distance is zero, and the bounds reduce to \(q\le P\lt q+1/(n+1)\), which contains the exact \(k/(n+1)\) of step 2. If the orders are independent, each distance is at most twice the total variation distance between calibration order \(i\) and the new order (their Lemma 1). Read the other way: with no ties, coverage more than \(1/(n+1)\) above \(q\) is possible only if the average swap distance exceeds the excess. In theory, then, over-coverage of that size signals non-exchangeability, but not which kind; the paper notes that a spread of \(Y\) given \(X\) that shrinks over time leads to over-coverage.

These bounds provide a theoretical reference. Estimating their distances requires the joint distribution of calibration and evaluation orders, which these data do not identify. The 96–97% is an observed proportion of rounded-up promises, not the probability in the theorem, which concerns the real-valued bound \(U\), and by step 3 equally \(\lfloor U\rfloor\). Ties void the upper bound, and the state rule's whole-day residuals tie often. The paper extends the lower bound to general scores such as this one-sided residual (its Appendix B); it states the upper bound for the absolute residual, and its proof (Appendix E.4) uses only that the scores are distinct, which is why I apply it here.

Covariate weighting addresses changes in the order mix, not general changes in delivery behaviour. Tibshirani, Barber, Candès and Ramdas [2] treat covariate shift. The calibration orders are drawn i.i.d. from \(P_X\times P_{Y\mid X}\); the new order is drawn independently from \(\tilde P_X\times P_{Y\mid X}\), so the features of new orders follow a different distribution while the delivery time given the features stays the same; and the predictor \(\widehat f\) is fixed, fitted on separate data. If \(\tilde P_X\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(P_X\) and \(w=d\tilde P_X/dP_X\), weighting each calibration residual by this likelihood ratio restores the guarantee (their Corollary 1, in the split form of their Section 2.2):

\[ \begin{aligned} U_w(x)&=\widehat f(x)\\ &\quad+Q_q\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i^w(x)\,\delta_{R_i}+p_{n+1}^w(x)\,\delta_{+\infty}\Bigr), \end{aligned} \]

where \(Q_q\) is the \(q\)-quantile of the weighted distribution and

\[ p_i^w(x)=\frac{w(X_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(X_j)+w(x)}, \] \[ p_{n+1}^w(x)=\frac{w(x)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(X_j)+w(x)}. \]

Then \(P\{Y_{n+1}\le U_w(X_{n+1})\}\ge q\). With \(w\equiv1\) this is the rule of step 2.

This sorts the causes listed above. A change in the order mix is covariate shift and, with the right weights, can be corrected. Faster or slower deliveries for the same kind of order change \(P_{Y\mid X}\): then this guarantee does not apply, and weighting by \(X\) alone is not guaranteed to restore coverage (Fig. 8 shows a case where it changes nothing). Selection that depends on the outcome is not covariate shift either.

In this project \(w\) is unknown. The paper estimates it with a classifier that tells calibration features from new ones: the odds \(\hat p(x)/\{1-\hat p(x)\}\) estimate \(w(x)\) up to the ratio of the two sample sizes, a constant that the normalisation in \(p_i^w\) cancels. The exact guarantee uses the true weights; evaluating estimated weights is a separate extension beyond E1.

Supplement: an idealised reference, the Beta distribution

Fix the fitted model and assume further that the calibration and future residuals are i.i.d. with a continuous distribution function \(F_R\). For a given calibration set, the true future coverage probability is

\[ p_C=P\{R_{\mathrm{new}}\le R_{(k)}\mid C\}=F_R\bigl(R_{(k)}\bigr). \]

Because the \(F_R(R_i)\) are independent uniform variables, as the calibration set is drawn again and again,

\[ p_C\sim\operatorname{Beta}(k,\,n+1-k), \]

so

\[ \mathbb E[p_C]=\frac{k}{n+1}, \] \[ \operatorname{Var}(p_C)=\frac{k(n+1-k)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}. \]

The distinction matters: once the calibration set is fixed, \(p_C\) is a fixed but unknown probability; the Beta distribution describes how \(p_C\) varies when the calibration set is redrawn [3].

With the project's \(n=17{,}056\) and \(q=0.9\), \(k=15{,}352\) and the standard deviation is about 0.230 percentage points.

This standard deviation describes repeated calibration sets under the i.i.d. continuous-score model. Evaluation uncertainty also involves finite test samples, order dependence, ties and temporal change. A drift test needs those sources of variation accounted for; dividing the observed coverage gap by 0.230% would use the wrong reference.

What I take from this

The quantile answers what to predict under an asymmetric loss. Conformal calibration answers how to build a marginal coverage guarantee under stated assumptions. Rounding decides how a prediction becomes an actual date. I evaluate the complete decision procedure:

  1. Prediction model
  2. Calibration rule
  3. Whole-day date
  4. Evaluation population

A lower model loss need not mean that this chain ends in better delivery promises. That is why the project reports days and coverage together.
