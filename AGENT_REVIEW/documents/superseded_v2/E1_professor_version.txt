When a lower forecast loss does not make a better delivery promise




  When a lower forecast loss does not make a
  better delivery promise
    Oct 20, 2026          · ​@geniussuzane


  1. Question and summary
  I started this project wanting to predict which Olist orders would arrive late. Along the
  way I realised that "late" compares two things: how long delivery takes, and how generous
  the promised date is. Thus a model can reduce lateness without making delivery faster.
  The question became: when does a better forecast actually give a better promised
  date?

  The data are the public Olist e-commerce orders (Brazil, 2017–2018). I study single-seller
  orders that were eventually delivered, so every result is conditional on delivery. A
  promised date is a whole number of days d; an order is covered if it arrives within d days.
  The target coverage is 90%. The evaluation set E has 19,950 orders purchased from April
  to July 2018.

  I compared a per-state quantile rule, a linear quantile regression and a shallow gradient-
  boosted model (HGB). All thresholds, data splits and candidate models were written
  down and hashed before any new model was fitted. Three findings:

   1. The comparison design changed the verdict. In the project's earlier comparison,
      regression promised 2.57 more days than the state rule. With the same training data,
      the same calibration procedure and the same feature timing, it promised 1.54 fewer
      days at 0.31 points lower coverage.
   2. A lower forecast loss did not give shorter dates. HGB had a 6.5% lower quantile loss
      than the linear model, yet its calibrated dates were 1.22 days longer.
   3. No new policy met the pre-set bar for replacing the existing rule. All calibrated
        policies over-covered E (96–97% against a 90% target).

  These results come from one evaluation period that the project had already analysed.
  Therefore this is a pre-registered exploratory study, not a blind test.

  2. Loss and decision: why a quantile, not the mean
  If a late day and a spare day cost different amounts, the best promise is a quantile of the
  delivery time, not its mean. Let Y be the delivery time in whole days and d the promise.
  Consider the simple loss

  L(d, Y) = c_late · (Y − d)₊ + c_slack · (d − Y)₊.

  The first term charges each late day; the second charges each unused day of slack. Let
  R(d) = E[L(d, Y)] and F the distribution function of Y. Moving the promise from d to d + 1
  saves c_late on every order with Y > d and adds c_slack on every order with Y ≤ d.
  Therefore

  R(d + 1) − R(d) = (c_late + c_slack) · F(d) − c_late.

  This difference never decreases in d. Thus R is minimised at the smallest whole d with
  F(d) ≥ q, where q = c_late / (c_late + c_slack). That is exactly the empirical quantile rule
  the project uses (NumPy's inverted_cdf). The pinball loss at level q is this loss with c_late
  = q and c_slack = 1 − q (scikit-learn). So a 90% target corresponds to a 9 : 1 penalty ratio.
  By contrast, the mean minimises squared error and the median minimises a 1 : 1 absolute
  loss; both treat a late day and a spare day alike.

  A check on validation data. On the 12,045 validation orders (July–September 2017), I gave
  every order the same promise and computed the 9 : 1 loss for d = 1, …, 60. The minimum is
  at d = 20, which is the empirical 90% quantile: F(19) = 89.6% < 90% ≤ F(20) = 91.4%.


     Promise                                        d (days)         On time   9 : 1 loss per order

     Mean (11.43), rounded up                                  12     65.5%                  23.76

     Median                                                    10     53.1%                  30.51

     Empirical 90% quantile                                   20      91.4%                  15.61


  What 9 : 1 does not mean. It follows from choosing q = 0.90; I did not measure it. It is not
  Olist's real cost ratio, and I have no cost data. Customer harm may not grow linearly with
  late days; if one late day already does most of the damage, a coverage constraint is closer
  to the truth than a linear cost. Costs may also differ by customer and product. Therefore
  the study reports the trade-off between coverage and promised days. It does not claim a
  profit-optimal date.

  3. Comparison design
  A fair comparison changes only the method. In the fair comparison, each calibrated policy
  therefore used the same training orders (F), the same label cut-off, the same rule for
  seller-history features and the same calibration procedure on C. The uncalibrated
  controls (B1 and the retained rule B0) are kept apart. A label counts only if the order was
  delivered before the decision date.


    Segment Purchases                       Labels known before Orders used Late rate* Role

    F0              Jan–Jun 2017            2017-12-01               13,616    3.89% fit candidates

    V               Jul–Sep 2017            2017-12-01               12,045    3.38% select settings

    F               Jan–Sep 2017            2018-04-01               25,666     3.67% final fit

    C               Oct–Dec 2017 2018-04-01                          17,056     8.74% calibration shift

    E               Apr–Jul 2018            —                        19,950    4.26% final scoring run


  *Against Olist's own estimated date.

  Calibration. Each calibrated policy is shifted by its own number computed on C: the k-th
  smallest of its residuals y − ŷ, with k = ⌈(n + 1) · q⌉ and n = 17,056. The promise is max{1, ⌈ŷ +
  shift⌉}. This has the form of split conformal, but its coverage guarantee needs C and E to
  be exchangeable. C had a much higher late rate than E, so I do not claim a guarantee
  (Barber et al.).

  Stepwise comparison. The project's earlier comparison (R0) differed in three conditions
  at once. So I changed one condition per step and recorded the gap "regression minus
  state rule" each time. The pre-set primary comparison is R3 against R0; R1 and R2 are
  descriptive.

  Under this fixed order, changing the design alone reversed the judgement. The largest
  step is R2, where the state rule receives the same C shift as the regression (+6 days for
  the state rule). This is a design-sensitivity result, not a causal statement about which
  method is better.

  Two references are kept apart. The fair reference (B1c: state rule fitted on F with the
  same C shift) asks whether a model adds value under equal conditions. The replacement
  reference (the project's retained state rule B0, fitted on F + C without a shift) asks
  whether the existing rule is worth replacing.


  4. Results
  The E1 models and decision rules were frozen before the final E1 scoring run, and each
  policy was scored once in that run; E had already been examined in Stage 7. A policy
  replaces another only if its mean promise is at least 1 day shorter and its coverage at most
  1 point lower, with no month of ≥ 1,000 orders more than 3 points below target. Intervals
  are 95% week-block bootstrap intervals (17 purchase weeks, 2,000 draws).

  5. Interpretation and limits
  Why the HGB dates are longer. HGB's uncalibrated integer-day pinball loss was about
  6.5% lower than the linear model's (1.324 against 1.417). However, its unrounded
  predictions were already about 0.90 days longer on average. The calibration shift then
  added about 0.31 days to the gap, and rounding added about 0.006 days.


     Days                                                 Linear C*          HGB D*    D* − C*

     Mean unrounded prediction                                  15.809        16.709   +0.900

     Shift computed on C                                             6.148    6.459     +0.311

     Rounding and the 1-day floor                                0.497        0.503    +0.006

     Mean promised days                                        22.453         23.671    +1.217


  Each value is rounded from full precision, so the rows may not add exactly. Thus lowering
  the quantile loss and shortening the average promise are different goals. Before
  calibration, the linear dates covered 88.33% of orders and the HGB dates 91.08%. Because
  the loss charges a late day nine times a spare day, longer dates with fewer misses can
  score better. This is not a reason to pick the uncalibrated HGB after the fact.

  Over-coverage. The policies that share the C shift step covered 96–97% against a 90%
  target. The uncalibrated controls (B1 and the retained B0) are outside this statement.

  This is consistent with calibration scores that changed over time. However, the late rate
  against Olist's own date fell from 8.74% on C to 4.26% on E, and that rate depends on
  delivery times, on how generous the original dates were, and on the mix of orders. This
  study has not separated the three. B0 also differs from the new policies in how it uses the
  training data (F + C, no shift). Therefore the conclusion is "do not replace in this round",
  not "the models or the calibration do not work". Calibration is the part most worth
  studying next, but I have not shown it is the main cause.

  Other limits.

        One evaluation period, already analysed in stage 7: a pre-registered exploratory study,
        not a blind test.
        Conditional population: single-seller orders that were eventually delivered.
        The week-block intervals use 17 weeks and describe fixed models only; they exclude
        refitting, tuning and selection uncertainty.
        The search over models was a small, stepwise one. It can miss changes that only help
        together.
        Promise dates do not change logistics. Nothing here shows faster delivery or happier
        customers.


  6. Conclusion
  This round does not support replacing the retained state rule. The more useful results are
  about method. First, a fair comparison rule changed the verdict between two simple
  methods. Second, a 6.5% lower forecast loss did not turn into shorter promises. Third, I
  accepted that the more complex model did not meet the bar I set before seeing the
  results. The open question is how well a calibration shift learned in one period carries over
  to the next. E1 ends here; that question would need a new, separately registered study.
