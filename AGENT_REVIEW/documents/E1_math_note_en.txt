Why a quantile sets the promised date, and where the calibration
guarantee stops
This note answers two questions. First, why the promised date is a quantile of delivery time
rather than its mean. Second, what “the coverage is met” means after calibration. I separate
three quantities that are easy to confuse: a proportion on the calibration set, a probability under
exchangeability, and a proportion observed in the evaluation period. The results used here are
standard: the quantile as the minimiser of an asymmetric linear loss, and the rank argument
behind split conformal calibration. I state and prove them in the form this project uses and then
apply them; none of them is new.

1. Why an asymmetric loss leads to a quantile
Let the delivery time be an integer Y ≥ 1 with E[Y ] < ∞, and the promise an integer d ≥ 1. A
promise that is too short makes the order late; one that is too long leaves the customer waiting. I
describe both costs with a linear loss,

                   L(d, Y ) = clate (Y − d)+ + cslack (d − Y )+ ,    clate , cslack > 0.

For a single order with information X = x at checkout, replace F by F (d | x) = P (Y ≤ d | X = x);
the optimal promise is then the conditional q-quantile. With clate = q and cslack = 1 − q, L is the
quantile (pinball) loss.
This loss gives the quantile target its decision meaning, but it is not every model’s fitting objective.
In the project the linear model fits a quantile regression on the log(1+Y ) scale and transforms back
to days; HGB fits on days directly; model selection and evaluation are on the day scale. Quantiles
are preserved under monotone transformations, so both scales aim at the same conditional quantile.
In a finite sample, however, the two fitting objectives differ.
The mean minimises squared loss, which treats a late day and a spare day as equally costly.
The mean does not generally minimise the asymmetric loss defined here; for some distributions it

This inequality uses no probability assumption, and it holds even if delivery conditions later change.
It is a necessary consequence of the specified calibration rule. Satisfying it alone neither verifies the
implementation nor establishes coverage on future orders: a shift wrongly set to 1,000 days would
satisfy it too. A more direct implementation check is to recompute the k-th residual independently
and compare it with the frozen shift.

The denominator is n + 1 because the new order is itself one of the n + 1 exchangeable draws. The
probability averages over which calibration set was drawn and which new order arrives. It does
not guarantee the target in every month or state, nor after one particular calibration set has been
observed. Random tie-breaking is used only in the proof; the algorithm is unchanged.

A simple example shows that the first and third quantities can come apart completely. The model
always predicts 10 days, and every calibration order arrives in 10 days, so s = 0 and ĉcal = 100%.
If future orders all take 15 days, ĉeval = 0. Conversely, if they all take 5 days, ĉeval = 100%, but
every order is promised 5 days too many. Proposition 2 holds in both cases; the exchangeability
behind Proposition 3 does not, so its guarantee does not carry over (Barber et al.).

3. Whole-day promises
A promise must be a whole number of days. Since Y is an integer, Y ≤ U ⇐⇒ Y ≤ ⌊U ⌋. Thus
rounding down covers exactly the orders that U covers; a minimum of one day can only enlarge
that set. The registered rule is max{1, ⌈U ⌉}. It covers every order that U covers, so the lower
bounds of Propositions 2 and 3 hold for it as well. Rounding U up to ⌈U ⌉ adds less than one day.
The minimum-one-day constraint is a separate adjustment and can only increase coverage.

4. Back to Olist
The calibration set has n = 17,056 orders and k = 15,352, so k/n ≈ 90.01% and k/(n+1) ≈ 90.00%.
For the linear model the three quantities are:

  • on the calibration set, the registered rounded-up promise covers 91.19%, not below k/n,
    consistent with Proposition 2; recomputing the k-th residual independently gives the frozen
    shift (6.1479 days);
  • the guarantee under exchangeability is at least k/(n + 1), but whether C (October–December
    2017) and the evaluation period E (April–July 2018) are exchangeable is itself the question;
  • in the evaluation period, the observed coverage on 19,950 orders is 96.18%.

The over-coverage on E suggests that the relation between the calibration and evaluation periods
deserves a closer look. It is compatible with changes in delivery times, with the sample selection,
and with changes in the order mix. This note runs no formal test of exchangeability and cannot
separate these contributions. Therefore I treat it as a diagnostic signal, not as confirmation of any
single cause. All conclusions apply only to single-seller orders that were eventually delivered.
