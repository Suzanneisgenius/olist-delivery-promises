Integer-valued delivery promises




Integer-valued delivery promises: calibration,
coverage and temporal shift
  ​Oct 21, 2026        · ​@geniussuzane


1. Summary
If two forecasting models go through the same calibration and the same rounding to
whole days, can their promised delivery dates be compared directly? This study finds that
a matched procedure is not enough on its own, and shows where the comparison still
breaks.

I started this project wanting to predict which Olist orders would arrive late. Along the
way I realised that "late" compares two things: how long delivery takes, and how generous
the promised date is. The question became: when does a better forecast actually give a
better promised date? The data are public Olist e-commerce orders (Brazil, 2017–2018).
The project's population keeps single-seller orders that were eventually delivered and
whose Olist estimated delivery week falls on or before 23 July 2018; Section 3 explains
why that last condition matters. A promise is a whole number of days; an order is covered
if it arrives in time; the target coverage is 90%.

I compared a per-state quantile rule, a linear quantile regression and a shallow gradient-
boosted model (HGB) under a protocol written down before any new model was fitted.
The design matched the training data, the calibration target and the rounding rule. Three
differences remained:

 1. The same calibration target did not give the same coverage. Every calibrated policy
     aimed at 90% on the calibration period. On the evaluation period the linear policy
     covered 96.2% with 22.45 days and HGB 97.1% with 23.67 days, so their registered
     points compare different reliability levels. A post-hoc diagnostic that aligns coverage
     using the evaluation outcomes puts HGB 0.6–0.9 days below the linear model at the
     four coverage targets reported, in this sample and for this family of shift policies.
 2. The same rounding rule treats continuous and whole-day bounds differently.
    Rounding the calibrated bound up adds less than a day of slack to a continuous
     forecast and none to the whole-day state rule: on the calibration period the continuous
     policies covered 91.1–91.2% against 90.3%. Rounding down instead keeps the coverage
     event of the bound (post hoc).
 3. The same evaluation sample is filtered on one comparator. The cohort is defined by
     Olist's estimated delivery week, which removes most July purchases and all later ones.
     This limits what the comparison with Olist's own promise can show.

and how calibration, integer rounding and cohort selection affect their interpretation.


2. Loss and decision: why a quantile, not the mean
If a late day and a spare day cost different amounts, the best promise is a quantile of the
delivery time, not its mean. Let Y be the delivery time in whole days and d the promise,
with the loss

L(d, Y) = c_late · (Y − d)₊ + c_slack · (d − Y)₊.

Let R(d) = E[L(d, Y)] and F the distribution function of Y. Moving the promise from d to d +
1 saves c_late on every order with Y > d and adds c_slack on every order with Y ≤ d.
Therefore

R(d + 1) − R(d) = (c_late + c_slack) · F(d) − c_late.

This difference never decreases in d, so R is minimised at the smallest whole d with F(d) ≥
q, where q = c_late / (c_late + c_slack). That is the empirical quantile rule the project uses
(NumPy's inverted_cdf). The pinball loss at level q is this loss with c_late = q and c_slack =
1 − q (scikit-learn); a 90% target thus corresponds to a 9 : 1 penalty ratio. The mean
minimises squared error and the median a 1 : 1 absolute loss; both treat a late day and a
spare day alike.

A check on validation data. On the 12,045 validation orders I gave every order the same
promise and computed the 9 : 1 loss for d = 1, …, 60. The minimum is at d = 20, the empirical
90% quantile: F(19) = 89.6% < 90% ≤ F(20) = 91.4%. Promising the mean (11.43, rounded
up to 12) would have covered only 65.5% of orders, at a loss of 23.76 per order against
15.61.

What 9 : 1 does not mean. It follows from choosing q = 0.90; I did not measure it, and it is
not Olist's real cost ratio. Customer harm may not grow linearly with late days; if one late
day already does most of the damage, a coverage constraint is closer to the truth than a
linear cost. Therefore the study reports the trade-off between coverage and promised
days. It does not claim a profit-optimal date.

3. Sample and comparison design
Sample. The population is defined on Olist's own estimated delivery week: an order enters
only if that week lies between 27 February 2017 and 23 July 2018, its final outcome is
delivered (on time or late), and it has a single seller. For the evaluation period E
(purchases from 1 April 2018) this keeps 19,950 of 32,803 purchases. The week condition
removes 5,175 of the 6,292 July purchases and every later one, so a late-window order
enters only if Olist promised it a short time. The evaluation sample is therefore filtered on
one of the comparators (Supplement S3c).

Matched design. Each calibrated policy used the same training orders, the same label
cut-off, the same rule for seller-history features and the same calibration procedure. A
matched procedure is not automatically neutral: rounding the calibrated bound up treats
a continuous forecast differently from a whole-day rule (Section 5). The uncalibrated
controls (B1 and the retained rule B0) are kept apart. A label counts only if the order was
delivered before the decision date.

Calibration. Each calibrated policy is shifted by its own number computed on C: the k-th
smallest of its residuals y − ŷ, with k = ⌈(n + 1) · 0.9⌉ and n = 17,056. The promise is then
rounded up: max{1, ⌈ŷ + shift⌉}. This has the form of split conformal, but its guarantee
needs C and E to be exchangeable, and C had a much higher late rate than E (Barber et
al.). Section 5 shows how this rounding treats continuous and whole-day predictors
differently.

The frozen HGB policy covered 97.10% with 23.67 days, the frozen linear policy 96.18%
with 22.45 days and B1c 96.49% with 23.99 days. Whole-day promises make coverage
move in steps, so every diagnostic comparison reports the coverage actually reached
rather than an interpolated match. At 90.02% HGB needs 16.60 days and the linear model
17.20; at 96.18% they need 21.71 and 22.44, while the state rule's nearest step is 96.49% at
23.99 days. In this sample and for this family of shift policies the HGB curve is the more
favourable one; that is not a general ranking of the two models. On C at the registered
shifts, HGB covered 91.14% with 24.54 days and the linear model 91.19% with 24.68.

The registered verdicts are unchanged. Intervals are week-block bootstrap intervals over

5. Rounding, sample window and calibration period
Four features of the design move the registered points. All numbers below are post hoc:
they reuse the frozen predictions, choose no new policy and change no registered
verdict.

Rounding to whole days. The registered promise rounds the calibrated bound U = ŷ +
shift up. Because Y is a whole number, Y ≤ U exactly when Y ≤ ⌊U⌋, so rounding down
keeps the coverage event of the untruncated bound, and the one-day minimum can only
enlarge it. Rounding up adds slack only when U is not a whole number, i.e. for continuous
forecasts. Rounding down would shorten each continuous promise by exactly 1 day on E
and leave B1c unchanged. Coverage on C would fall from 91.1–91.2% to 90.01%, which still
meets the rank bound, and HGB would be 1.32 days shorter than B1c at +0.18 points. A
plain "subtract one day" is not coverage-preserving in general: it can exclude orders that
sit exactly at a whole-number bound (Supplement S3b). The registered rule is therefore
conservative rather than wrong, and the registered H3 verdict stands.

Evaluation window. July contributes 1,085 orders, with Olist promises of at most 26 days
(56–63 in April–June) and a mean actual time of 6.2 days. In April–May alone, coverage is
95.1% for the linear policy, 96.4% for HGB and 95.5% for B1c; in June–July it is 98.3–98.5%.

Coverage is higher in the later, more heavily selected months. This is consistent with a
contribution from the window restriction, but the analysis does not separate it from
changes over time or in the order mix. The comparison with Olist is also filtered on the
comparator itself.

Calibration period. C runs from October to December 2017. Late rates were 4.2%, 12.5%
and 7.4% by month, and November holds 42% of C, so a shift learned there may over-
cover a calmer period. This is a candidate explanation; a high November late rate does not
by itself show that it caused the over-coverage.

Replacement standard. A policy may lose up to 1 point of coverage but gets no credit for
extra coverage. At B0's own coverage (93.95%) on E, the diagnostic curves need 19.12 days
for HGB and 19.83 for the linear model, against 20.31 for B0. The verdict says that the
registered policies missed the standard, not that the models lack decision value.

These four points do not overturn the registered results. They change how the results
should be read: each registered difference mixes forecast quality with calibration level,
rounding and sample window, and this study does not separate those parts.


6. Limits and conclusion
Limits. One evaluation period, already examined earlier in the project and filtered on
Olist's estimated week; a conditional population (single-seller, eventually delivered
orders); a small stepwise model search; approximate intervals from 17 week clusters; no
separate test of seasonality. Promised dates do not change logistics, so nothing here
shows faster delivery or happier customers.

Conclusion. The pre-registered verdicts stand: the matched design reversed the
regression-versus-rule judgement, V selected no new linear setting, HGB did not clear the
1-day bar, and the registered policies did not meet the replacement standard. The
sensitivity checks narrow what these verdicts mean. The frozen policies reached
different coverage, rounding up gave continuous forecasts extra slack, and the evaluation
window was filtered on Olist's own dates. The next step is a small, separately recorded
methods study: how to turn a continuous calibrated bound into a whole-day promise
(rounding down and its boundary cases), and a paired simulation that separates rounding,
temporal shift and selective labels. Online recalibration is left for later, because Olist
labels arrive late and the first orders to be delivered are not a random subset.
